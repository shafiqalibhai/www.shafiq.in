Spirituality

Notes on religion, philosophy, meditation, and the practical side of meaning-making. Covers Ismaili tradition, Stoicism, existentialism, and various contemplative practices.

I’ve been thinking a lot about spirituality lately. Not in the abstract, philosophical sense — more like the practical, daily kind. The kind that shows up when you’re sitting in a room full of people and feel completely alone, or when you’re walking through a forest and suddenly remember to breathe.

This page is where I’m collecting my thoughts on the subject. It covers religion, philosophy, meditation, and everything in between. Some of it is personal, some of it is research, and some of it is just me trying to figure things out as I go.

Beliefs#

Religion#

Shia Islam#

I was raised Shia Muslim, specifically in the Ismaili tradition. It’s not something I think about every day, but it’s always there — like the background music in a film you can’t quite hear but know is playing.

Shia Imami Ismaili Muslim#

The Ismailis are a branch of Shia Islam that traces its leadership through the Imams, starting with Ali ibn Abi Talib and continuing in an unbroken line. For me, this isn’t just historical trivia. It’s a living connection — a sense that I’m part of something much bigger than myself, stretching back over a thousand years.

The current Imam, Prince Karim Aga Khan IV, is someone I look to for guidance. Not in a blind-following way, but more like how you might look to a mentor. He’s given me a framework for thinking about faith, service, and how to live in a modern world without losing your spiritual centre.

Ginans#

Ginans are devotional hymns that form part of the Ismaili spiritual tradition. They’re written in various languages — Sindhi, Hindi, Gujarati, Urdu — and they blend Islamic theology with local poetic forms. When I hear them sung, there’s something about the melody that gets under your skin.

They’re not just religious texts. They’re stories, prayers, and philosophy all wrapped into one. Some of my favourite ginans tell the stories of prophets, others explore the nature of the soul, and a few are just straight-up beautiful love poems — to God, obviously, but they read like anything but.

Quasidahs#

Quasidahs are another form of devotional poetry in the Ismaili tradition. They’re more structured than ginans — usually written in a specific meter and rhyme scheme. I find them less accessible than ginans, but there’s a discipline to them that I respect. They remind me that spirituality doesn’t have to be loose and free-form to be meaningful.

Nandi#

Nandi is a prayer of thanksgiving, usually said at the end of a gathering or ceremony. It’s a moment of collective gratitude — everyone in the room saying the same words at the same time. There’s something powerful about that synchronicity, even if you’re not deeply religious. It makes you feel like you’re part of a community, even if just for a few minutes.

Awal Sufro#

Awal Sufro is the first evening of Ramadan, when Ismailis gather for a special prayer and sermon. It’s one of those nights that marks the beginning of something. The atmosphere in the jamatkhana changes — there’s a sense of anticipation, like everyone is holding their breath together.

Festivals#

There are several festivals in the Ismaili calendar, each with its own significance:

  • Milad — The birthday of the Prophet Muhammad and his family. A time for reflection and celebration.
  • Jashn-e-Berez — The spring equinox celebration, marking the new year in the Zoroastrian calendar. It’s one of those festivals that reminds me how many layers of tradition are woven into Ismaili practice.
  • Eid-e-Milad-un-Nabi — Another celebration of the Prophet’s birthday, observed with special prayers and gatherings.
Miraj#

Miraj refers to the Night Journey of the Prophet Muhammad, when he is believed to have travelled from Mecca to Jerusalem and then ascended through the heavens. For Ismailis, this event has deeper esoteric meanings — it’s not just about the physical journey but about the soul’s ascent towards divine understanding.

I first learned about Miraj as a child, and it always struck me as one of those stories that sits somewhere between history and metaphor. Is it meant to be taken literally? Maybe. But even if it’s symbolic, the idea of a journey through different states of consciousness is pretty powerful.

Yawm e Ali#

Yawm e Ali, or the Day of Ali, commemorates the event at Ghadir Khumm where the Prophet Muhammad is said to have designated Ali as his successor. It’s a significant day in Shia Islam and one that reinforces the importance of leadership and succession in our tradition.

For me, it’s less about the politics of succession and more about the idea of commitment — to a cause, to a community, to something larger than yourself.

Waez#

A waez is a sermon or religious discourse, usually given by a learned member of the community. I’ve attended many waezis over the years, and they vary enormously in quality. Some are genuinely moving — the kind that stay with you for days. Others feel like they’re going through the motions.

The best waezis are the ones that connect ancient texts to modern life. When a speaker can make you feel like a thousand-year-old verse is speaking directly to your current situation, that’s when religion feels alive.

Khidma#

Khidma means service, and it’s a core value in the Ismaili tradition. The Aga Khan has always emphasised the importance of serving your community and your country, regardless of your faith. It’s not just a nice idea — it’s practically woven into the fabric of Ismaili institutions.

I’ve done my fair share of khidma over the years, from volunteering at local events to helping with community projects. It’s not always glamorous, but it gives you a sense of purpose that other things rarely do.

Month of Rajab#

Rajab is the seventh month of the Islamic calendar and holds special significance for Ismailis. It’s a time of reflection, prayer, and preparation for the months ahead. The first evening of Rajab is marked with special gatherings and prayers.

I’ve noticed that during Rajab, there’s a different quality to the community — people seem more contemplative, more inclined to reach out to others. It’s a small thing, but it makes the month feel distinct from the rest of the year.

Shukravari Beej#

Shukravari Beej is the first Friday of Navroz (the Ismaili New Year). It’s celebrated with special prayers and gatherings, and it marks the beginning of the new year in the Farvardin calendar. The word “shukravari” comes from the Persian word for Friday, and “beej” means seed — so it’s something like the seeding of the new year.

I’ve always liked the imagery of it. There’s something hopeful about planting a seed and trusting that it will grow.

Laylat al-Qadr#

Laylat al-Qadr, the Night of Power, is considered the holiest night in the Islamic calendar. It commemorates the night when the Quran was first revealed to the Prophet Muhammad. For Ismailis, it’s a time of intense prayer and reflection.

I remember spending many Laylat al-Qadr nights in the jamatkhana, sitting in the dark, listening to prayers being recited. There’s a particular kind of peace that comes from being in a room full of people who are all focused on the same thing at the same time.

Salgirah#

Salgirah is the celebration of an Imam’s birthday. It’s one of the most important events in the Ismaili calendar, and it’s marked with special prayers, gatherings, and feasts.

I used to wonder why we celebrate the Imam’s birthday when birthdays aren’t really a traditional Islamic practice. But then I thought about it differently: birthdays are a way of expressing love and gratitude for someone who matters to you. If the Imam is someone who matters deeply to the community, then celebrating his birth makes sense — not as a religious obligation, but as an expression of devotion.

Philosophy#

Stoicism#

Stoicism has been a useful framework for me, especially during difficult periods. The core idea is simple: some things are up to us, and some things aren’t. Focus on what you can control, accept what you can’t, and don’t waste energy worrying about the rest.

It’s not a particularly original insight, but it’s surprisingly hard to actually practice. I find myself returning to Stoic texts when life feels chaotic — usually Seneca or Marcus Aurelius.

Epictetus#

Epictetus was a former slave who became one of the most influential Stoic philosophers. His teachings, recorded by his student Arrian in the Discourses and the Enchiridion, are remarkably practical. He doesn’t deal in abstract metaphysics — he talks about how to live.

One of my favourite passages from the Enchiridion is the opening: “Some things are up to us, and some things are not up to us.” Everything that follows flows from that simple distinction.

Seneca#

Seneca was a statesman, playwright, and Stoic philosopher who lived in Rome during the first century AD. His letters to his friend Lucilius — collected as the Moral Letters to Lucilius — are some of the most readable philosophical writing ever produced.

What I appreciate about Seneca is his honesty about human weakness. He doesn’t pretend that wisdom is easy or that we’ll never slip up. He writes about frustration, anger, grief, and fear as if he’s experienced them himself — because he had.

Existentialism#

Existentialism appeals to me because it takes responsibility seriously. The existentialists — Sartre, de Beauvoir, Camus — argued that we are radically free and therefore radically responsible for our choices. There’s no predetermined essence, no cosmic plan, no excuse.

That can be terrifying, but it’s also liberating. If life has no inherent meaning, then I get to decide what meaning has. It’s a heavy burden, but it’s mine.

Jean-Paul Sartre#

Sartre is the existentialist most people have heard of, and for good reason. His idea that “existence precedes essence” — that we exist first and then define ourselves through our actions — is both simple and devastating.

I’ve always been struck by his honesty about the anxiety that comes with freedom. He didn’t pretend that being free was comfortable. It’s not. But he argued that it’s unavoidable, and that pretending otherwise is what he called “bad faith.”

Simone de Beauvoir#

De Beauvoir was a sharper thinker than Sartre, in my opinion. Her Second Sex is still one of the most important works of feminist philosophy, and her ethical writings — especially The Ethics of Ambiguity — offer a more nuanced take on existentialism than Sartre’s.

What I appreciate about de Beauvoir is her attention to the concrete details of lived experience. She doesn’t just talk about abstract freedom — she talks about what it feels like to be constrained by gender, by class, by circumstance.

Humanism#

Humanism, broadly speaking, is the view that human beings are capable of leading ethical and meaningful lives without reference to supernatural beliefs. It’s not anti-religious — many humanists are religious — but it does place the focus on human agency and human experience.

I find humanism appealing because it takes human suffering seriously without offering easy answers. It doesn’t promise that everything happens for a reason. It says: things are hard, but we can make them better, and that’s enough.

Renaissance Humanism#

Renaissance humanism was a cultural and intellectual movement that emphasised the study of classical texts, the importance of education, and the potential of human beings. It emerged in Italy in the fourteenth century and spread across Europe.

What strikes me about Renaissance humanists is their optimism about human potential. They believed that through education and self-improvement, people could achieve greatness. It’s a refreshing contrast to some of the more pessimistic philosophical traditions.

Secular Humanism#

Secular humanism is the modern offshoot of humanist thought. It explicitly rejects religious belief and instead grounds ethics in human reason and experience. Prominent secular humanists include Bertrand Russell, Carl Sagan, and Margaret Atwood.

I appreciate secular humanism’s commitment to evidence and reason, but I also think it can sometimes underestimate the role that ritual, community, and transcendence play in human life. Not everyone needs a God to find meaning, but not everyone is satisfied by reason alone either.

Meditation#

Transcendental Meditation (TM)#

TM is a specific form of mantra meditation that was popularised in the West by Maharishi Mahesh Yogi. Practitioners sit comfortably with their eyes closed and silently repeat a mantra — a specific sound or phrase — for about twenty minutes, twice a day.

I’ve tried TM on and off over the years. It’s not particularly difficult, but it does require consistency. The benefit I’ve noticed is subtle: a general sense of calm that seems to linger after practice, rather than any dramatic spiritual experience.

Mantra Meditation#

Mantra meditation involves repeating a word, phrase, or sound — either aloud or silently — to focus the mind. It’s found in many traditions: Hinduism, Buddhism, Sikhism, and even some Christian contemplative practices.

What I like about mantra meditation is its simplicity. You don’t need any special equipment or training — just a mantra and a few minutes of quiet. The challenge is keeping the mind from wandering, but that’s true of most meditation practices.

Yogic Flying#

Yogic Flying is a practice associated with the Divine Life Society and the Maharishi Mahesh Yogi. Practitioners sit on the ground and gently bounce or float, supposedly generating cosmic energy.

I’ll be honest — I’m sceptical about Yogic Flying. It looks strange, and the claims made about it seem exaggerated. But I suppose if it works for people, who am I to judge? Meditation is personal, and what works for one person might not work for another.

Loving-kindness Meditation#

Loving-kindness meditation, or metta meditation, involves cultivating feelings of goodwill and compassion towards oneself and others. You typically start by directing loving-kindness towards yourself, then towards a loved one, then towards a neutral person, then towards a difficult person, and finally towards all beings.

I find this practice genuinely moving. It’s easier to feel compassion for a stranger than for someone you have a complicated relationship with, which is probably the point. The practice reveals how conditional our kindness often is.

Metta Bhavana#

Metta bhavana is the Pali term for loving-kindness meditation. It’s a central practice in Theravada Buddhism and has been adapted by many secular mindfulness programmes.

The practice involves systematically extending feelings of metta (loving-kindness) from yourself outwards to increasingly wider circles of beings. It’s a way of training the mind to be more compassionate, and research suggests it can actually change how people respond to others.

Tonglen#

Tonglen is a Tibetan Buddhist meditation practice that involves breathing in suffering and breathing out compassion. It’s a powerful practice, but it’s not for beginners — it can be emotionally intense.

The idea is that by willingly taking in the suffering of others (symbolically, through the breath), you develop compassion and reduce your own ego-centredness. It’s a counterintuitive practice, but one that many people find deeply transformative.

Zen Meditation#

Zen meditation, or zazen, is the practice of sitting in silence and observing the mind without attachment. There’s no mantra, no visualisation — just sitting, breathing, and watching thoughts arise and pass away.

I’ve tried zazen a few times, and I always come away feeling like I didn’t do it “right.” But that might be the point. Zen is less about achieving a particular state and more about seeing clearly what’s already there.

Zazen#

Zazen is the Japanese term for seated meditation in the Zen tradition. Practitioners sit in a specific posture — usually cross-legged on a cushion, with hands resting in the lap and eyes half-closed. The focus is on posture and breath, not on any particular technique.

What I appreciate about zazen is its simplicity. There’s nothing to achieve, nothing to attain. You just sit. The difficulty is in the sitting — in letting go of the constant urge to do something, to fix something, to become something.

Koan Practice#

A koan is a riddle or paradox used in Zen meditation to provoke doubt and transcend logical thinking. The most famous koan is “What is the sound of one hand clapping?” — but there are thousands of others.

Koan practice is meant to short-circuit the analytical mind and lead to direct insight. I’ve never been able to “solve” a koan, but I’ve found that sitting with one for a while can be surprisingly relaxing. It forces you to stop thinking so hard about everything.

Connection#

Nature#

I’ve always felt a particular kind of peace in nature. It’s not just the scenery — though that helps — but the sense of scale. When you’re standing in a forest or on a mountaintop, your problems feel smaller, not because they’ve changed but because everything else has expanded.

Outdoor Retreats#

I’ve done a few outdoor retreats over the years — some spiritual, some secular. The common thread is that they remove you from your normal environment and force you to pay attention to something other than your daily routine.

The best retreats are the ones that don’t try too hard. You don’t need elaborate programmes or charismatic leaders. Sometimes all you need is a quiet place, some company, and time to think.

Forest Bathing#

Forest bathing, or shinrin-yoku, is a Japanese practice of spending time in the forest for health and well-being. It’s not about exercise or hiking — it’s about being present in the forest, engaging all your senses.

I tried it once on a walk through a woodland near my house, and I was surprised by how different it felt from a normal walk. When you slow down and actually pay attention — to the smell of the leaves, the sound of the wind, the quality of the light — the forest becomes a different place.

Wilderness Survival#

I’ve never taken a wilderness survival course, but I’ve always been drawn to the idea. There’s something appealing about being able to provide for yourself in a natural environment — to find shelter, start a fire, purify water.

It’s not just about practical skills, though. I think wilderness survival also teaches you something about your own capacity to cope with difficulty. When you’re stripped of your modern conveniences, you discover what you’re actually made of.

Eco-tourism#

Eco-tourism is travel that minimises environmental impact and supports local communities. I’ve done a bit of eco-tourism over the years — staying in lodges, visiting national parks, volunteering on conservation projects.

What I’ve learned is that travel doesn’t have to be destructive. You can see the world without leaving a trail of carbon and waste, if you’re willing to slow down and pay attention.

Sustainable Travel#

Sustainable travel is about making choices that reduce your environmental impact while travelling. It can be as simple as taking a train instead of a plane, or as complex as offsetting your carbon footprint through verified programmes.

I’m not perfect at this — I still fly more than I should — but I’ve been trying to be more conscious about it. Every trip is a chance to make better choices, even if they’re small.

Wildlife Conservation#

Wildlife conservation is important to me, both as a practical concern and as a moral one. The rate at which species are going extinct is alarming, and it’s largely driven by human activity.

I’ve supported various conservation organisations over the years, and I try to be informed about the issues. It’s easy to feel powerless in the face of something as large-scale as biodiversity loss, but individual actions add up — and collective action starts with individual awareness.

Nature Walks#

Nature walks are my go-to form of exercise and meditation. I don’t need a trail or a summit — a patch of green is enough. There’s something about walking in nature that clears my head in a way that walking in a city never does.

I’ve noticed that the best walks are the unplanned ones. The ones where you just put on your shoes and go, without a route or a destination in mind.

Birdwatching Tours#

I’ve never been a serious birdwatcher, but I’ve done a few tours over the years and found them surprisingly enjoyable. There’s something calming about trying to identify birds by their song or silhouette — it forces you to slow down and pay attention.

If I were to get more into birdwatching, I’d probably start with a good field guide and spend time in my local park. You’d be surprised how many species you can find if you just look.

Photography Walks#

Photography walks combine walking with photography, usually in a natural or urban setting. I’ve done a few of these, both as part of organised groups and on my own.

What I’ve learned is that photography changes the way you see. When you’re looking for a good shot, you notice things you would otherwise miss — the way light falls on a wall, the pattern of leaves on the ground, the expression on a stranger’s face.

Community#

Religious Communities#

Religious communities have been important to me throughout my life. The jamatkhana — the Ismaili place of worship — has been a constant source of support, friendship, and belonging.

What I’ve learned from religious communities is that belief alone isn’t enough. You need a community to practice your beliefs with, to challenge you, to support you, and to celebrate with you. Faith is not a solo endeavour.

Monastic Communities#

I’ve never lived in a monastic community, but I’ve visited a few and spent time with monks and nuns from various traditions. What strikes me about monastic life is the intentionality — every aspect of daily life is designed to support spiritual growth.

It’s not a lifestyle I could sustain, but I admire the commitment. There’s something admirable about choosing to simplify your life in service of something larger than yourself.

Spiritual Retreats#

Spiritual retreats have been a recurring feature of my life. Some have been religious — pilgrimages, prayer gatherings, fasting periods. Others have been secular — mindfulness retreats, meditation intensives, yoga holidays.

The common thread is that they all remove you from your normal routine and give you space to think, breathe, and be. I’ve found that even a weekend retreat can shift your perspective in ways that weeks of ordinary life don’t.

Social Groups#

Social groups — whether formal or informal — are essential for well-being. Research consistently shows that strong social connections are one of the best predictors of happiness and longevity.

I’ve learned this the hard way. There have been periods in my life when I was socially isolated, and I can tell you that it takes a toll — on your mental health, your physical health, and your sense of purpose.

Interest-based Clubs#

Interest-based clubs are a great way to meet people who share your passions. I’ve been part of book clubs, hiking groups, photography societies, and more. What I’ve found is that shared interests are a surprisingly effective icebreaker.

You don’t need to have deep conversations with someone to enjoy their company. Sometimes you just need to agree on something — a book, a trail, a film — and build from there.

Volunteer Organizations#

Volunteering has been a significant part of my life, both through Ismaili institutions and through secular organisations. It’s one of those things that sounds simple but has a profound impact — on the people you help and on yourself.

I’ve learned that volunteering is not about saviourism. It’s about solidarity — recognising that we’re all in this together and doing what we can to make things better.

Support Groups#

Support groups have been invaluable for me at various points in my life. Whether it’s a grief support group, a mental health group, or a recovery group, the shared experience of struggle creates a bond that’s hard to find elsewhere.

What I’ve learned from support groups is that vulnerability is not weakness. Sharing your struggles with others doesn’t make you a burden — it makes you human, and it often helps others who are going through similar things.

Recovery Groups#

Recovery groups — whether for addiction, grief, or other life challenges — are built on the idea that sharing your story can be healing. The twelve-step tradition, in particular, emphasises the importance of sponsorship, community, and service.

I’ve attended a few recovery meetings over the years, and what strikes me is how honest people are. There’s no pretence, no performance — just people telling the truth about their lives. That honesty is rare and precious.

Grief Support Groups#

Grief support groups are for people who have lost someone close to them. They’re not always easy to attend — grief is raw and uncomfortable — but they can be incredibly helpful.

What I’ve learned from grief support groups is that grief doesn’t follow a timeline. There’s no right way to grieve, and there’s no point at which you’re “done.” You learn to carry your loss with you, and over time, it becomes part of who you are.

Inner Self#

Self-reflection#

Self-reflection is the practice of examining your thoughts, feelings, and behaviours. It’s something I’ve tried to do regularly, though not always successfully.

I find that journaling is one of the most effective forms of self-reflection. Writing down your thoughts forces you to slow down and organise them, and it often reveals patterns you wouldn’t notice otherwise.

Meditation Retreats#

Meditation retreats are intensive periods of practice, usually lasting from a few days to several weeks. They’re challenging — sitting in silence for extended periods can bring up difficult emotions — but they’re also transformative.

I’ve done a few meditation retreats, and each one has taught me something different. The common thread is that silence is not empty. When you stop filling every moment with noise, you start to hear what’s been there all along.

Solo Travel#

Solo travel has been one of the most valuable experiences of my life. There’s something about being alone in an unfamiliar place that forces you to confront yourself. You can’t hide from your thoughts when there’s no one else around.

I’ve travelled solo through Europe, Asia, and Africa, and each trip has taught me something about who I am and what I want. The lessons are rarely what you expect — they’re usually about your own reactions, not the places you visit.

Spiritual Retreats#

I’ve already mentioned spiritual retreats, but they deserve a second mention because they’re so important. Whether religious or secular, retreats give you the space to step outside your normal life and pay attention to what matters.

I’ve found that the best retreats are the ones that don’t programme every minute. Some of my most valuable moments have been in the unstructured time — walking alone, sitting in silence, writing in a journal.

Silent Retreats#

Silent retreats are exactly what they sound like — periods of silence, usually lasting from a day to several weeks. You don’t speak, you don’t use your phone, you don’t read or write. You just sit, walk, and eat in silence.

I did a three-day silent retreat once, and it was one of the hardest and most rewarding things I’ve ever done. By the second day, my mind was racing — full of thoughts, memories, anxieties. By the third day, it had settled into something quieter. I didn’t have any profound revelations, but I did have a sense of peace that lasted for weeks afterwards.

Vision Quests#

Vision quests are a Native American practice involving a period of solitude in nature, often accompanied by fasting, to seek spiritual guidance. I’ve never done a vision quest, but I understand the appeal — there’s something primal about spending time alone in the wilderness and asking the big questions.

I think many people do informal vision quests without realising it — periods of solitude, reflection, and connection with nature. You don’t need a cultural framework to benefit from sitting alone in the woods and thinking about your life.

Mindfulness Practices#

Mindfulness is the practice of paying attention to the present moment without judgment. It’s been popularised in recent years, but it has roots in many spiritual traditions.

I’ve found mindfulness useful for managing anxiety and staying present. It’s not a cure-all, but it’s a tool — like any tool, it works better with practice.

Walking Meditation#

Walking meditation is the practice of bringing mindfulness to the act of walking. You pay attention to each step, to the sensation of your feet touching the ground, to the movement of your body.

It’s simpler than it sounds, but harder than it looks. I’ve found that walking meditation is particularly useful when sitting meditation feels impossible — when my mind is too restless to be still.

Eating Meditation#

Eating meditation involves bringing full attention to the act of eating. You notice the colours, textures, flavours, and smells of your food. You eat slowly, without distraction.

It sounds trivial, but it’s surprisingly difficult to eat without scrolling through your phone or watching TV. When you actually pay attention to your food, it becomes a more satisfying experience — and you tend to eat less because you’re actually aware of when you’re full.

Body Scan Meditation#

Body scan meditation involves systematically bringing attention to different parts of your body, usually starting at your toes and working your way up to your head. It’s a form of mindfulness that helps you notice tension, discomfort, or relaxation in different parts of your body.

I’ve found body scan meditation useful for sleep — it helps me relax and let go of the day’s tensions. It’s also a good way to check in with yourself, to see where you’re holding stress or emotion.

Practices#

Prayer#

Prayer is a practice I’ve maintained throughout my life, though the form it takes has changed. As a child, prayer was something I did because I was told to. As an adult, it’s something I do because I want to — a way of connecting with something larger than myself.

I’ve found that prayer doesn’t have to be formal or structured. Sometimes it’s a ritualised recitation, sometimes it’s silent contemplation, and sometimes it’s just sitting quietly and thinking about what matters to you.

Daily Prayers#

Daily prayers are a central practice in Islam, with five prescribed prayer times throughout the day. For Ismailis, the form and timing may vary, but the principle remains — prayer as a regular practice of remembrance and connection.

I’ve tried to maintain a daily prayer practice for most of my adult life, though there have been gaps. What I’ve learned is that consistency matters more than perfection. It’s better to pray a little every day than to pray a lot once in a while.

Morning Prayers#

Morning prayers set the tone for the day. I’ve found that starting the day with a few minutes of prayer or meditation helps me feel grounded and focused.

There’s something about the quiet of the morning — before the world gets loud — that makes it a good time for reflection. Even if you only have five minutes, it’s enough to centre yourself before the day begins.

Evening Prayers#

Evening prayers are a way of closing the day — of reflecting on what happened, expressing gratitude, and letting go of what you can’t control.

I’ve found evening prayers particularly useful during difficult periods. They give me a chance to process what I’ve been through and prepare for the next day.

Rituals#

Rituals are structured practices that mark important moments in our lives. They can be religious — like baptism, confirmation, or marriage — or secular — like birthdays, anniversaries, or graduations.

What I’ve learned from rituals is that they matter. They give us a way of marking time, of creating meaning, of connecting with something larger than ourselves. Even if you don’t believe in the supernatural aspects of a ritual, the psychological benefits are real.

Religious Ceremonies#

Religious ceremonies — whether weddings, funerals, or festivals — are important social and spiritual events. They bring people together, mark transitions, and reinforce shared values.

I’ve attended many religious ceremonies over the years, and I’ve noticed that the ones that resonate most are the ones that feel authentic. You can tell when a ceremony is being performed out of habit versus when it’s being performed with genuine feeling.

Baptism#

Baptism is a Christian ritual of purification and initiation, usually involving water. It’s not part of my own tradition, but I’ve attended many baptisms and have always been struck by the significance people attach to them.

There’s something universal about the idea of washing away the old and starting fresh. It’s a powerful metaphor, regardless of your religious beliefs.

Confirmation#

Confirmation is a Christian rite of passage, usually occurring in adolescence, in which a person affirms the faith commitments made on their behalf at baptism. It’s a moment of personal commitment — a way of saying “this is my choice, not just my parents’.”

I appreciate the idea behind confirmation, even though I don’t practice it. The notion that faith should be a personal choice, not just an inherited tradition, is one I agree with.

Celebrations#

Celebrations — whether religious, cultural, or personal — are important for well-being. They give us a reason to pause, to connect with others, and to mark the passage of time.

I’ve learned that celebrations don’t need to be grand or expensive. A dinner with friends, a walk in the park, a quiet evening at home — these can be just as meaningful as a big event.

Festivals#

Festivals are communal celebrations that mark important dates in the religious or cultural calendar. They bring people together, reinforce shared identity, and provide a break from ordinary routine.

I’ve celebrated many festivals over the years — Eid, Navroz, Christmas, Diwali — and I’ve always appreciated the sense of continuity they provide. Festivals connect us to our past and to each other.

Rites of Passage#

Rites of passage mark important transitions in life — birth, coming of age, marriage, death. They’re found in every culture and religion, and they serve an important function: they help us navigate change.

What I’ve learned from rites of passage is that transitions are harder when they’re unmarked. Having a ritual to mark a change — even a small one — gives it weight and meaning.

Mindfulness#

Daily Mindfulness Practices#

Daily mindfulness practices can be simple — a few minutes of breathing, a moment of gratitude, a walk without your phone. The key is consistency, not duration.

I’ve found that even small mindfulness practices can have a significant impact on my day-to-day life. They don’t eliminate stress or problems, but they help me respond to them more skillfully.

Mindful Breathing#

Mindful breathing is the practice of paying attention to your breath. It’s the simplest form of meditation and one of the most effective.

When I’m feeling anxious or overwhelmed, I often return to mindful breathing. It’s a way of anchoring myself in the present moment, of reminding my body that I’m safe.

Mindful Eating#

I’ve already mentioned mindful eating, but it deserves emphasis because it’s one of the easiest ways to practice mindfulness. Every meal is an opportunity to be present — to taste your food, to notice your hunger and fullness, to appreciate the effort that went into preparing your meal.

Mindful Movement#

Mindful movement includes practices like yoga, tai chi, and qigong — activities that combine physical movement with mental attention. They’re a good way to bring mindfulness into your body, not just your mind.

I’ve found mindful movement particularly useful for managing stress and improving focus. It’s also a reminder that mindfulness isn’t just about sitting still — it can be practiced in motion.

Tai Chi#

Tai chi is a Chinese martial art that’s practised as a form of moving meditation. It involves slow, deliberate movements combined with deep breathing and mental focus.

I’ve tried tai chi a few times and found it relaxing, though I’m not skilled enough to do it properly. The idea — that movement can be a form of meditation — is one I find compelling.

Qigong#

Qigong is similar to tai chi but focuses more on breath and energy work. It’s a Chinese practice that combines movement, breathing, and meditation to cultivate qi (life energy).

I’ve found qigong to be a gentle form of exercise that also has a meditative quality. It’s not as physically demanding as other forms of exercise, but it leaves me feeling calm and centred.

Yoga Asanas#

Yoga asanas are the physical postures practised in yoga. They’re often the most visible aspect of yoga in the West, but they’re just one part of a much larger practice.

I’ve done yoga on and off for many years, and I’ve found it useful for both physical and mental well-being. The postures improve flexibility and strength, and the emphasis on breath and awareness has a calming effect.
