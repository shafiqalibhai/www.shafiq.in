Self Promotion at Work

How to get credit for the work you do without being the kind of person people resent. A practical guide to visibility, alliances, and not making things awkward.
5 min · Shafiq Alibhai

I used to think self-promotion meant bragging. Turns out it just means making sure the right people know what you’ve done. There’s a difference, and learning it changed how I move through my career.

The people who get promoted aren’t always the best workers. They’re the ones whose work gets seen. That’s not a conspiracy — it’s just how organisations work. If nobody knows you shipped the thing that saved the quarter, you’re not going to get the promotion.

So here’s what I’ve picked up about getting credit for the work you actually do.

Own Your Words#

The easiest place to start is with language. When you talk about something you did, say “I” not “we”. Not because collaboration doesn’t matter — it does — but because “we” erases you. Your manager can’t advocate for someone they can’t identify.

This isn’t about taking credit for other people’s work. It’s about being precise. If you wrote the report, say you wrote the report. If you led the project, say you led it. You can acknowledge help without swallowing your contribution whole.

Make Your Manager Your Ally#

Your manager is the single most important relationship in your career at any given company. Not because they have power over you, but because they’re the one who talks about you when you’re not in the room.

Keep them informed. Send updates. Show them what you’ve done before they have to ask. A manager who knows your work can fight for you; a manager who doesn’t know your work can’t.

And if your manager isn’t doing this for you, that’s data. It tells you something about the relationship, and maybe about the organisation.

Use Performance Reviews Properly#

Yearly reviews are one of those institutional rituals everyone goes through, but most people treat them as a formality. Don’t. This is your moment to sit across from someone with authority and lay out exactly what you’ve delivered.

Prepare a document beforehand. List your achievements with dates, context, and impact. Not “I improved the process” but “I redesigned the onboarding workflow in March, which cut setup time from two weeks to three days.” Specificity is what separates a strong review from a forgettable one.

You’re not asking for permission to feel good about your work. You’re giving your manager the ammunition they need to make a case for you.

Be Visible Without Being Loud#

Speaking up in meetings matters, but there’s a difference between contributing and dominating. The goal isn’t to be the loudest voice — it’s to be the one people remember.

Ask thoughtful questions. Offer a perspective that shifts the conversation. Follow up after with an email summarising what you proposed, so there’s a paper trail and your name attached to the idea.

And make sure you’re engaging with the right people. Not just your immediate team, but the folks a few levels up who influence hiring and promotion decisions. A casual coffee chat or a well-timed question after a presentation can plant a seed.

Make Your Boss Look Good#

This sounds counterintuitive if you’re focused on yourself, but it’s one of the most effective strategies there is. When your manager succeeds, you succeed with them.

Share credit where it’s due, yes, but also share your results upward. If you delivered something significant, loop your manager in. Frame it as “we” when appropriate — the win is theirs too — but make sure your contribution is visible within that frame.

A manager who feels supported by you will advocate for you. A manager who feels threatened by you won’t. Read the room.

Build Alliances#

You don’t need to be everyone’s favourite, but you do need allies. People who’ve seen your work, trust your judgment, and are willing to mention your name in the right context.

Set up regular 1 on 1s with people across the organisation, not just your direct team. Learn what they’re working on. Offer help where you can. These relationships compound over time.

When the promotion conversation comes up, having three people who can vouch for you carries more weight than one manager doing all the talking.

Don’t Be the Person Who Makes Things Awkward#

None of this matters if you’re the colleague people resent. Don’t embarrass coworkers in public. Don’t steal credit for other people’s work — that reputation travels faster than anything else. Be competent, be reliable, and don’t make people regret supporting you.

Self-promotion has a ceiling. Cross it and you become someone nobody wants to work with, and no amount of visibility will save you.

Define What You’re Actually Trying to Get#

Before you invest energy in any of this, ask yourself what you want. A promotion? More interesting work? Higher pay? Different responsibilities?

The strategy changes depending on the answer. If you want to move up, visibility and alliances matter most. If you want better projects, making your manager look good and speaking up in the right forums will get you there. If you just want to do good work without the drama, sometimes the best promotion strategy is to be so reliable that people can’t ignore you.

Know your target. Everything else is just tactics.
