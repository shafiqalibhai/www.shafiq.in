Books that actually helped me with the people side of project management

  4. Drive — Daniel H. Pink
  5. The Five Dysfunctions of a Team — Patrick Lencioni
  6. Influence — Robert B. Cialdini

  8. The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People — Stephen R. Covey

I spent a chunk of last year reading my way through the soft-skills section of the bookshop. Not because I’d suddenly become interested in personal development — more because I was struggling with a project where the technical bits were fine but everything around it kept falling apart. Miscommunications. Teams that wouldn’t align. Stakeholders who seemed to be operating in a different reality.

So I started reading. Not the textbook stuff, but the books people actually recommend when they’re being honest. Here’s what stuck.

How to Win Friends and Influence People — Dale Carnegie#

Yes, it’s old. Yes, everyone recommends it. But it’s worth reading because it’s the original version of everything that came after it, and Carnegie had a genuine gift for saying complicated things simply.

The bit that stayed with me wasn’t any particular technique — it was the underlying attitude. The idea that most problems with other people come from not making them feel valued, and that the simplest way to fix it is to actually be interested in them. Not performative interest. Real interest.

I’ve gone back to this one more times than I care to admit, usually when a conversation isn’t going where I want it to.

Emotional Intelligence 2.0 — Travis Bradberry and Jean Greaves#

This one’s more of a workbook than a read-through. It gives you a test, tells you your score in four areas — self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship management — and then gives you strategies for improving each one.

The strategies are practical without being naive. Things like “when someone criticises you, don’t react immediately — count to ten first” sound obvious until you’re in the moment and your default is to fire back. The book helps you build habits that kick in before your instincts do.

I’d say this is the most immediately useful one on the list if you’re serious about changing how you behave, not just understanding why you behave the way you do.

Crucial Conversations — Kerry Patterson et al.#

This book is about the moments that matter. The conversations where emotions run high, opinions differ, and the stakes are real. Miss these and projects derail. Handle them well and everything else becomes easier.

The framework they offer — making it safe for people to talk, getting to the heart of the matter without attacking, finding shared purpose — isn’t revolutionary. But it’s structured in a way that makes it actually usable when you’re in the room and your pulse is racing.

I’ve used the “start with heart” technique more than once: before a difficult conversation, ask yourself what you actually want, not just what you want to win. It changes the whole tone of the exchange.

Drive — Daniel H. Pink#

Pink’s argument is straightforward: the old model of motivation — rewards and punishments — doesn’t work for anything that requires creative or cognitive effort. Autonomy, mastery, and purpose are what actually move people.

I found this one particularly useful because it gave me a language for things I’d been observing but couldn’t quite articulate. The team member who stopped caring after three years wasn’t lazy — they’d lost autonomy over their work. The person who lit up when given a hard problem wasn’t a nerd — they’d found mastery.

It changed how I think about managing people, even if I don’t always have the power to give them everything they need.

The Five Dysfunctions of a Team — Patrick Lencioni#

Written as a fable, which sounds pretentious but actually works. You follow a CEO trying to save his company, and through the story you see the five things that kill teams: absence of trust, fear of conflict, lack of commitment, avoidance of accountability, and inattention to results.

The book is simple — some would say too simple — but simplicity is the point. These dysfunctions aren’t rare. They’re the default state of most teams, especially project teams that are thrown together and expected to perform.

I keep coming back to the first one: trust. Not the corporate kind, but the vulnerable kind — the ability to admit when you’re wrong, ask for help, or say you don’t know. Without that, nothing else follows.

Influence — Robert B. Cialdini#

This is the book that made me more careful about how I communicate and more aware of how others try to communicate with me. Cialdini identifies six principles of persuasion — reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking, and scarcity — and explains how each one works.

I don’t read this for tips on manipulating people. I read it to recognise when those principles are being used on me, and to make sure I’m applying them ethically when I’m trying to get buy-in for something.

The reciprocity principle alone has saved me from more bad deals than I can count.

Leaders Eat Last — Simon Sinek#

Sinek’s argument is that great leaders create circles of safety — environments where people feel secure enough to take risks, share ideas, and push each other without fear of humiliation.

The title comes from a Marine Corps tradition where officers eat last so that enlisted personnel are fed first. It’s a small detail, but it captures the whole idea: leadership is about putting the people you’re responsible for before yourself.

I’ve never been a fan of Sinek’s broader TED-talk style, but this book has substance. The biology stuff — oxytocin, endorphins, the chemical basis of trust and belonging — is interesting even if you’re not convinced by the management angle.

The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People — Stephen R. Covey#

I know, I know. It’s everywhere. But I read it relatively late and it was genuinely useful, especially the distinction between private victory (winning over yourself) and public victory (working effectively with others).

The habit of “begin with the end in mind” is the one I use most. Before starting a project, before a meeting, before sending an email — ask yourself what the outcome should be. It sounds basic but most people operate in reaction mode, responding to whatever’s loudest rather than whatever matters.

Thinking, Fast and Slow — Daniel Kahneman#

This one isn’t a management book. It’s a psychology book about how we think, written by a Nobel laureate. But it’s essential reading for anyone who makes decisions — which is to say, everyone.

Kahneman describes two systems: System 1, which is fast and intuitive, and System 2, which is slow and deliberate. Most of our decisions are made by System 1, and most of those are wrong in predictable ways.

The biases he documents — anchoring, confirmation bias, the sunk cost fallacy — aren’t just academic. I’ve seen projects continue long after they should have been killed because everyone involved had already invested too much time in them. I’ve seen budgets inflated because the first number mentioned set the anchor. Understanding these patterns doesn’t eliminate them, but it makes you slower to accept your first instinct.

Mindset — Carol S. Dweck#

The fixed vs. growth mindset distinction has been absorbed into so much pop psychology that it’s almost meaningless now. But the original research is solid, and the implication for leadership is significant.

People with a fixed mindset believe their abilities are static. People with a growth mindset believe they can develop them. The difference shows up everywhere — in how people respond to feedback, how they handle failure, how much risk they’re willing to take.

As a manager, the question is: are you modelling a fixed mindset or a growth mindset? When was the last time you said “I don’t know” out loud? When was the last time you asked for feedback and actually acted on it? Your team is watching.

What I didn’t read#

There are plenty of other books on this topic that I haven’t got to — High Output Management by Andy Grove, The Manager’s Path by Camille Fournier, Multipliers by Liz Wiseman. Maybe next year.

The common thread in the ones I have read is that soft skills aren’t soft. They’re the hard part of management, the part that can’t be learned from a Gantt chart or a risk register. The technical skills get you the job. The people skills determine whether you keep it — and whether the work actually gets done.
