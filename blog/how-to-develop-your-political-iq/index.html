How to develop your political IQ

Table of Contents

  4. Read the room
  5. Know when to walk away
  6. The bottom line

Most people treat office politics as something to avoid. They’d rather focus on their work and hope it speaks for itself. But the truth is, everyone is already playing the game — whether they admit it or not. The difference is between people who understand how it works and people who get surprised by it.

Here’s what I’ve learned about developing political awareness at work.

Know where you’re going#

You can’t navigate an office if you don’t know what you’re looking for. What do you actually want? A promotion? A transfer to a different team? More influence over decisions? Be honest with yourself about it.

Once you have a sense of direction, you start noticing things you’d otherwise miss. Who makes decisions in this company? Who do people actually listen to, even if they don’t have a fancy title? Which projects get funding and which ones get forgotten?

Pay attention to who matters#

Every organisation has an informal hierarchy that exists alongside the org chart. There’s the person everyone goes to for advice, the one who has the boss’s ear, the one who’s been around long enough to know where all the bodies are buried.

This isn’t about flattering powerful people. It’s about understanding the landscape so you’re not caught off guard. When a decision comes up that affects your work, you’ll know who to talk to — and who to avoid annoying.

Build real relationships, not contacts#

There’s a difference between collecting contacts and actually knowing people. A contact is someone you mention on LinkedIn. A relationship is someone who’d return your call at 10pm because they genuinely like you.

The best way to build relationships is to be useful. Help someone with a problem. Share information that might help them. Offer to cover for them when they’re swamped. These small gestures compound over time.

Read the room#

People don’t always say what they mean. A colleague who agrees with your proposal in the meeting but then stalls on implementation is telling you something. A manager who praises your work publicly but never puts your name forward for opportunities is telling you something else.

You don’t need to be paranoid about it. But developing the habit of noticing what people do rather than just what they say will save you a lot of headaches.

Know when to walk away#

Sometimes the politics are too toxic to work within. If you’re constantly second-guessing yourself, if good work is punished and sycophancy is rewarded, no amount of political IQ will fix that. The smartest move in those situations is often to leave.

But before you do, make sure you’re leaving for something, not just from something. Have a plan. Use what you’ve learned about the organisation’s dynamics to find a place where you’ll actually thrive.

The bottom line#

Political IQ isn’t about manipulation. It’s about awareness. It’s understanding that work is done through people, and people are complicated. The better you understand that complexity, the better your chances of getting what you want — and keeping your sanity while doing it.
