Some notes on people skills and emotional intelligence

Table of Contents

  2. Assertiveness

I’ve been reading up on emotional intelligence lately and collecting the bits that felt useful. Not the textbook definitions — the ones that actually change how you show up in a room.

The five things that matter#

There’s a framework that keeps coming up, and it boils down to five skills: rapport building, curiosity, communication, ambition, and conflict resolution. They’re not glamorous. They’re not the sort of things you put on a CV under “technical expertise”. But they’re the difference between being someone people want to work with and someone they tolerate.

Rapport is the foundation. It’s not about being liked — it’s about being approachable. The people who build rapport well don’t try harder; they make others feel easier.

Curiosity is the engine. Not the performative kind where you nod along and wait for your turn to speak, but the genuine sort that makes people feel heard. Ask better questions and you’ll get better answers.

Communication is the delivery mechanism. Most people think this means speaking well. It doesn’t. It means making sure what you meant is what they understood.

Ambition is the fuel. Not the cutthroat kind, but the quiet determination to get better at the work and at the people around you. Without it, the other four skills have no direction.

Conflict resolution is the pressure test. Anyone can get along when things are easy. The skill is knowing how to navigate disagreement without burning the bridge.

Assertiveness#

This is the one I’ve been working on most. Assertiveness isn’t aggression, and it isn’t passivity either. It’s the ability to speak and act in a way that makes people lean in rather than tune out.

The trick I’ve found is to lead with agreement before you disagree. “You could be right about that…” or “I understand how you feel…” — these aren’t surrender phrases. They’re pacing mechanisms. They get the other person to lower their guard so your actual point has somewhere to land.

The diary exercise#

There’s a technique that sounds simple but is genuinely uncomfortable: keep a diary of your own aggressive and passive behaviour. Not other people’s. Yours.

Every time you snapped at someone, wrote that down. Every time you stayed quiet when you should have spoken, wrote that down too. The point isn’t to beat yourself up — it’s to see the pattern. Most people have one dominant mode and they don’t even notice it until it’s staring back at them on a page.

I’ve found that passive behaviour tends to show up as missed opportunities. Aggressive behaviour shows up as damaged relationships. Both are expensive in different ways.

What I’m still figuring out#

Emotional intelligence isn’t a destination. It’s more like a muscle you keep training and occasionally forget to use when it matters most. The reading helps, the frameworks help, but the real work happens in the moments you don’t prepare for.
