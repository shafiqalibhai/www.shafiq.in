Bathukamma

12 October 2024 · 3 min · 468 words · Shafiq Alibhai
Table of Contents
  1. How it works
  2. Where it comes from

Bathukamma is a flower festival from Telangana. Women stack seasonal flowers into tiered arrangements, sing songs while dancing around them, and at the end of each day float them in water. It runs for about ten days in the autumn, usually September or October, and coincides with the end of the monsoon when flowers are at their peak.

The name comes from “Bathuku Amba” — may she live long — and is directed at Goddess Gauri. It’s not a festival with a single grand ceremony. It’s something that happens in neighbourhoods, courtyards, and public squares across Telangana, with each group making their own arrangement and singing their own songs.

How it works#

Each day has a theme and a specific arrangement. The first day, Polla Bathukamma, uses wild flowers and is the simplest arrangement. Over the following days the stacks get taller and more elaborate. The final day, Jaitra Bathukamma, is the largest.

The flowers are arranged in concentric circles on a flat base, usually made from leaves or a plate. Marigold, dahlia, sunflower, and jasmine are common. Each layer is a ring of flowers placed stem-outward, then the next layer on top is smaller in diameter. The result looks like a stepped tower or a small temple.

Women gather in groups, make the Bathukamma together, sing traditional songs called Bathukamma paddalu, and dance around the arrangement. The songs are in Telugu and vary by region. At dusk the Bathukamma is carried to a nearby pond, lake, or tank and set afloat.

Where it comes from#

The festival has ancient roots. Some accounts link it to Sati, the wife of Shiva, whose body parts fell at different sites across India — those sites became Shakti Peethas. Telangana is associated with one of these sites. Other accounts trace it to harvest celebrations, which makes sense given the autumn timing.

The festival was largely a rural tradition until the 1990s. As people moved to cities like Hyderabad, they brought it with them. Now it’s celebrated in urban neighbourhoods too, and the Telangana government has promoted it as part of the state’s cultural identity since the state was formed in 2014.

What makes it different from other flower festivals#

Onam in Kerala uses flower carpets (pookalam) on the ground. Bathukamma is three-dimensional — the flowers are stacked vertically. The songs are also specific to the festival and to Telangana, rather than being general devotional songs. And the floating of the flowers at the end is a distinct ritual that doesn’t have a direct parallel in other Indian flower festivals.

I find it interesting that a festival built around something as ephemeral as cut flowers has lasted for centuries. The arrangements are beautiful for a day, then they’re released into water. There’s nothing permanent about it, which seems to be the point.
