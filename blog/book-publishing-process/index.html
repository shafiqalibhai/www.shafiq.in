Book Publishing Process

The stages of publishing a book, in rough order:

  1. Writing — research, drafting, revising until you have a complete manuscript.
  2. Editing — developmental editing for structure and narrative, then line editing for prose, then copyediting for grammar and consistency.
  3. Proofreading — final pass for typos and formatting errors before anything goes to print.
  4. Design — cover design and interior layout (fonts, margins, page breaks).
  5. Production — printing or e-book formatting. Offset lithography for large runs, digital printing for shorter ones.
  6. Marketing — promotional materials, advertising, media outreach, social media.
  7. Distribution — getting the book to readers via publishers, online retailers, bookshops, or direct sales.

Writing#

Start with a concept, do the research, outline the structure, then draft. The first draft doesn’t need to be good — it needs to exist. Revision comes after. Get feedback from beta readers or a critique group before you consider the manuscript finished.

Editing#

Developmental editing addresses the big picture: plot structure, character arcs, pacing. Line editing refines sentence-by-sentence. Copyediting catches grammar, spelling, and consistency. These are separate passes, not one sitting.

Proofreading#

A final check for typos, formatting issues, and cross-references. Read the text aloud or use a different font to spot errors your eye has learned to skip. Tools like Grammarly help but don’t replace a human read-through.

Design#

The cover is the first thing people see. Research covers in your genre to understand what works. For interior design, serif fonts (Times New Roman, Baskerville) are standard for fiction; sans-serif works for some non-fiction. Line spacing of 1.5x the font size is comfortable for extended reading.

Production#

Binding options: perfect bound (glued spine, softcover), case bound (hardcover), saddle stitched (stapled, for shorter books), or spiral bound (for reference materials). Quality control catches misaligned pages, print defects, and binding issues before packaging.

Distribution channels#

  • Traditional publishers handle distribution but take a share of royalties and creative control.
  • Self-publishing (Amazon KDP, Smashwords, Apple Books) gives full control but puts marketing on you.
  • Online retailers (Amazon, Barnes & Noble) offer reach and advertising tools.
  • Independent bookshops serve niche audiences; Bookshop.org routes orders to them.
  • Wholesale distributors (Ingram, Baker & Taylor) get books into chains, libraries, and international markets.
  • Direct sales at events or through your own website maximise margins but limit reach.

Pre-launch#

Set up product pages on retailers with accurate metadata. Secure pre-orders. Send advance review copies through NetGalley or directly to bloggers. Prepare press materials: author bio, book summary, high-res cover image.

Post-launch#

Monitor sales and reviews. Keep marketing going — the launch week is not the end of promotion. Author events, email newsletters, and targeted ads sustain momentum. Use sales data to inform your next project.
