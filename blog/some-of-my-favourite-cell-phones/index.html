Some of my favourite cell phones

23 January 2012 · Shafiq Alibhai

There was a period, roughly 2004 to 2008, when phone manufacturers decided that phones could be fashion accessories. Not phones that looked like fashion accessories. Actual fashion accessories that happened to make phone calls. I collected a few of them, or at least got close enough to handle them, and I still think some of the best phones I’ve ever held had nothing to do with specs or benchmarks.

Nokia 8800 Sirocco#

This was the one that started it for me. Stainless steel body, a sliding mechanism that felt like opening a luxury watch case, and a weight to it that told you it was built from something real. The Nokia 8800 Sirocco wasn’t trying to be a smartphone. It was trying to be a thing you pulled out of your pocket and knew, immediately, was expensive. It worked.

Nokia 8600 Luna#

The 8600 had this smoky glass front that caught light in a way I still can’t describe properly. Press a key and the keypad lit up from beneath — not the harsh white glow of a backlight, but something warmer, almost liquid. It was the kind of detail that made you look at your phone differently. Art and technology, sure, but said without the usual marketing sneer.

LG KE850 Prada#

Before the iPhone made touchscreens normal, the Prada made them desirable. Neill Barrett designed it for Prada, and you could feel the design philosophy in every curve. It was thin, it was black, it was the kind of phone that made people ask what it was before they even knew it made calls. I still think it’s one of the most beautiful phones ever made, and that’s not just nostalgia talking.

Nokia 8800 Arte#

The Arte was the 8800’s bigger sibling, and where the Sirocco was understated, the Arte was confident. Scratch-resistant steel, an OLED screen that made colours look like they were glowing from inside the glass, and a build quality that made cheaper phones feel like toys by comparison. It wasn’t just pretty. It was well-made, which is rarer.

Samsung Serenata#

Bang & Olufsen and Samsung got together and made a phone for people who cared about how something sounded as much as how it looked. The scroll wheel was satisfying in a way that has nothing to do with technology. You’d spin it and think, someone actually designed this. The sound quality was genuinely good for a phone of that era, which says something.

Motorola Aura#

A circular screen. Actual Swiss watch movements inside. The Aura was Motorola saying, we can make a phone that belongs on a wrist, and somehow they almost pulled it off. It was heavy, it was fragile, and it was absolutely ridiculous. I loved it for that.

LG KF750 Secret#

Carbon fiber body, slide-out keypad, and one of the first 5-megapixel cameras in a phone that didn’t feel like a camera with a phone attached. The Secret had substance underneath the style, which was refreshing. You could use it as a camera, a music player, and a phone without feeling like you’d compromised on any of them.

Nokia 7900 Prism#

Geometric patterns, customizable LED lights on the back that you could program to flash in different patterns. It was quirky in a way that felt intentional rather than desperate. The Prism didn’t try to be everything to everyone. It was a phone for people who liked things that looked different.

O2 Cocoon#

The Cocoon had an LED display on the outside that showed the time, notifications, even a small alarm clock function. You didn’t need to open it to check the time. It was a small idea, but the execution was clean, and it made you think about what a phone could do beyond being a slab of glass and metal you opened and closed a hundred times a day.

Samsung Armani#

Giorgio Armani and Samsung. Sleek, dark, expensive-looking. The Armani was a phone that did what it said on the tin: it looked expensive. Nothing more, nothing less. And sometimes that’s exactly what you want.

Nokia 8800 Carbon Arte#

The Carbon Arte took the Arte and swapped the steel backplate for carbon fiber. Lighter, still sturdy, and with a texture that felt different every time you held it. If the Arte was confident, the Carbon Arte was composed. Subtle upgrade, but you noticed it.

Nokia 8800 Gold Arte#

Gold-plated. Extravagant. The Gold Arte wasn’t a phone you bought for the phone. It was a phone you bought because you could. Status symbol is the right term, even if it’s reductive. It was a statement, and it made no apologies for being one.

LG BL40 New Chocolate#

Tall, thin, widescreen. The BL40 was built for watching videos and listening to music, and its unusual shape made it stand out in a sea of rectangles. Not everyone’s cup of tea, but it had character.

Sony Ericsson XPERIA Pureness#

Transparent display. Yes, really. The Pureness was a marvel of engineering in the same way a glass-blowing competition is a marvel of engineering: impressive, slightly absurd, and unlikely to survive contact with reality. It drew attention. That was the point.

Samsung M7500 Emporio Armani#

Another Armani collab, this time with Emporio instead of the main line. Slightly more approachable, slightly less pretentious, but still a phone that looked like it belonged in a boutique. The M7500 proved that the Armani-Samsung partnership wasn’t a one-off. It was a formula, and a decent one.

Looking back, most of these phones would be useless today. No app stores, limited web browsing, cameras that would embarrass a calculator. But they were honest about what they were: objects. Things you held, looked at, admired. Before smartphones turned phones into pocket computers, there was room for a phone to just be a well-made object that happened to connect you to people.

I miss that, sometimes. Not the phones specifically — my current phone does things those couldn’t even dream of. But the idea that a phone could be something you appreciated for its design, its materials, its craft, rather than its processing speed.
